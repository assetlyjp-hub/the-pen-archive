If you spend any time around fountain pens, you will eventually run into "the big three" — Sailor, Pilot, and Platinum. These are the three Japanese houses that, between them, define what a Japanese fountain pen feels like. I have owned and used pens from all three for years, and the more I write with them, the clearer it becomes that they are not interchangeable. Each one has a personality, and once you understand those personalities, choosing between them gets a lot easier.

This is the comparison I wish someone had handed me when I started. I will walk through who these companies are, how their nibs actually feel in the hand, the filling systems they favor, their signature models, the all-important question of how Japanese nib sizing works, and who each brand really suits. There is a comparison table and an FAQ at the end, plus the honest downsides nobody likes to mention.

## A Quick Word on Heritage

All three companies were founded within eight years of each other, in the early days of Japan's modern manufacturing era.

**Sailor** is the eldest, founded in 1911 in Hiroshima. It was Japan's first fountain pen maker, and that "first" matters to how the company thinks of itself. Sailor leans into craft and nib artistry. Their master nibmeister tradition — the famous Nagahara lineage of specialty nibs — is the clearest expression of that.

**Pilot** arrived in 1918 in Tokyo and grew into the largest of the three by a wide margin. Pilot is the engineer of the group. If a clever mechanism exists in the fountain pen world, Pilot has probably built a version of it — retractable nibs, vacuum fillers, you name it.

**Platinum** followed in 1919, also in Tokyo. Platinum is the quiet, slightly underrated one. They are stubborn about a few things — dry, precise nibs and a sealing cap that keeps ink from drying out — and those stubborn choices turn out to be exactly why a lot of people love them.

## Nib Character: The Heart of the Difference

This is the single most important section, because the nib is where you actually feel the difference. Forget the spec sheets for a moment — here is what each one is like to write with.

### Sailor: Feedback and "Pencil-Like" Texture

Sailor nibs give you **feedback**. That is the word everyone uses, and it is the right one. When you write with a Sailor, you can feel the paper texture coming up through the nib — a fine, controlled scratch that many people (me included) find quietly addictive. It is not roughness or scratchiness in the bad sense; it is more like the difference between a sharp pencil and a felt-tip. You always know exactly where the tip is.

Sailor's smaller nibs in particular (their fine and medium-fine) write thin, crisp lines that are wonderful for small Japanese characters and for tight English handwriting. Their 21K gold nibs are a bit softer and add a touch of bounce, but the signature feedback never fully disappears, and that is the point.

### Pilot: Smooth and Springy

Pilot nibs are the **smooth** ones. Their gold nibs glide. The higher-end models — the Custom 823, the Custom 743 — are often described as writing on glass, and that is not marketing. There is very little friction between you and the paper.

Pilot also has a reputation for **spring**. Many of their gold nibs have a soft, responsive quality, and a few are designed specifically to flex. If you ever want to dip a toe into line variation, Pilot's soft and Falcon nibs are the friendliest on-ramp of the three brands.

### Platinum: Crisp and Dry

Platinum sits at the third corner of the triangle: **crisp and on the dry side**. Their nibs lay down a more controlled, less wet line than the other two. For some writers that is a downside; for a lot of us it is the whole appeal. A drier nib means sharper lines, faster dry times, and far less feathering and bleed-through on cheap office paper.

Platinum's flagship 3776 nib has a slightly firm, precise feel — not as smooth as a Pilot, not as feedback-forward as a Sailor, but clean and confident. If your handwriting is small or your paper is bad, Platinum is often the one that makes you look best.

For more on how the metal itself shapes feel, see [gold nib vs steel nib](/magazine/gold-nib-vs-steel-nib).

## Filling Systems

How a pen takes on ink matters more for daily life than people expect. Here is where each brand stands.

- **Sailor** mostly keeps it simple: proprietary cartridges and a converter on the vast majority of pens. The Pro Gear and 1911 lines are cartridge/converter. It is fuss-free, easy to clean, and easy to travel with — but ink capacity is modest, and you are tied to Sailor's cartridge shape (or any bottle, via the converter).
- **Pilot** is the variety act. You will find cartridge/converter pens (the CON-70 converter is a genuinely great one), the **vacuum-filling** Custom 823 that holds a huge reservoir of ink, and the retractable Vanishing Point. Pilot's CON-40 converter, found on cheaper models, has a smaller capacity that some people grumble about.
- **Platinum** is mostly cartridge/converter as well, but with their headline trick layered on top — the **Slip & Seal** cap (more on that below). Their cartridges are a touch larger than Sailor's, which is a small but real convenience.

If filling mechanisms are new to you, our [fountain pen filling systems guide](/magazine/fountain-pen-filling-systems-guide) breaks them all down.

## The Signature Models

Every one of these brands has a "this is us" pen. Knowing them is shorthand for knowing the brand.

### Sailor: Pro Gear and 1911

The **Pro Gear** (flat-ended) and the **1911** (rounded, cigar-shaped) are Sailor's twin pillars. Mechanically they are nearly identical — same nibs, same filling system — so the choice is purely about which silhouette you like. Both come in a "Slim" size for smaller hands and a full-size standard. These are the pens that show off Sailor's feedback nib, and they are where most people meet the brand. (See `sailor-pro-gear`.)

### Pilot: Custom 823 and Vanishing Point

The **Custom 823** is Pilot's enthusiast darling — a vacuum filler with an enormous ink capacity, a semi-transparent amber or smoke body so you can watch your ink level, and one of the smoothest gold nibs in the business. (See `pilot-custom-823`.)

The **Vanishing Point** (a.k.a. Capless) is the other side of Pilot's brain: a fully retractable fountain pen with a real gold nib, deployed with a click like a ballpoint. It is one of the most genuinely innovative pens ever made. I cover it in depth in the [Pilot Vanishing Point review](/magazine/pilot-vanishing-point-review-2026).

### Platinum: 3776 Century and Slip & Seal

The **3776 Century** is Platinum's flagship, named for the height of Mount Fuji in meters. Its claim to fame is the **Slip & Seal** mechanism — an inner cap that seals tight when you post the cap, dramatically slowing ink evaporation. In practice this means you can leave a Platinum 3776 capped and idle for weeks, even a couple of months, and it will still write on the first stroke. No other big-three pen does this as well. If you are the kind of person who rotates between many pens and lets some sit, the 3776 is built for you. (See `platinum-3776-century`.)

## The Thing Nobody Warns Beginners About: Japanese Nib Sizes Run Fine

Here is the single most common surprise for newcomers to Japanese pens. **Japanese nib sizes run finer than Western ones.** A Japanese Medium writes roughly like a Western Fine. A Japanese Fine writes like a Western Extra-Fine. A Japanese Extra-Fine can be needle-thin.

This is by design — Japanese writing has many fine strokes packed into small spaces, so the whole industry calibrates finer. All three of the big three follow this convention.

What it means for you:

- If you are used to European pens and you like a Medium, buy a **Japanese Broad** to get a similar line.
- If you write small, take a lot of notes, or use cheaper paper, the naturally fine Japanese nibs are a gift.
- If you love a wet, juicy, expressive line, you may find stock Japanese fine nibs a little stingy — look to Broad or specialty grinds.

I go deeper on this in [how to choose nib size](/magazine/how-to-choose-nib-size), which is worth reading before you buy your first Japanese pen.

## Price Tiers

Roughly speaking, here is where these brands land. Prices vary by region and retailer, but the relative ordering is stable.

- **Entry gold (around $130–200):** Platinum 3776 Century and Sailor Pro Gear Slim / 1911 Slim sit at the friendly end of gold-nib territory. The 3776 in particular is frequently the cheapest way into a genuinely excellent 14K gold nib. These show up constantly in roundups of the [best fountain pens under $200](/magazine/best-fountain-pens-under-200).
- **Mid gold (around $200–360):** Full-size Sailor Pro Gear / 1911, Pilot Custom 74 and Custom 823, and the Pilot Vanishing Point all live here.
- **Higher end ($400+):** Sailor's larger King of Pen, Pilot's Custom 845 and urushi pieces, and Platinum's celluloid and maki-e models climb well past this.

All three also make excellent steel-nib pens far below this (Pilot Metropolitan, Platinum Preppy and Plaisir, Sailor's lower lines), which is where most people should actually start.

## Who Each Brand Suits

**Choose Sailor if** you want a tactile, characterful writing experience and you enjoy *feeling* the page. Sailor rewards people who slow down and notice the act of writing. Great for fine writers, small handwriting, and anyone who finds glass-smooth nibs a little boring.

**Choose Pilot if** you want smoothness, reliability, variety, and clever engineering. Pilot is the safest first recommendation of the three — there is a Pilot for every budget and every preference, and they almost never disappoint out of the box. If you want flex, retractability, or huge ink capacity, only Pilot delivers.

**Choose Platinum if** you want a crisp, precise line, the best ink-preservation in the business, and excellent value at the gold-nib entry point. Platinum is the pen for the person with many pens, the person who writes small, and the person stuck using mediocre office paper.

## Honest Downsides

No brand is perfect, and pretending otherwise helps no one.

- **Sailor** had a meaningful price increase over the past several years, and its lower-end converters are nothing special. The feedback that fans love is genuinely off-putting to people who want pure smoothness — try before you commit if you can.
- **Pilot's** budget converters (the CON-40) have small capacity and can be finicky, and the Vanishing Point's clip placement falls right under some people's grip. The sheer breadth of Pilot's lineup can also be paralyzing for a first-time buyer.
- **Platinum's** dry nibs can feel *too* dry to writers who like a wet line, and the brand's styling is more conservative — fewer flashy finishes than the other two. Their lowest-end pens (Preppy) feel cheap in the hand, even if they write well.

## FAQ

**1. Which of the big three is best for a complete beginner?**
Pilot, most often. The Metropolitan (steel) and Custom 74 (gold) are forgiving, smooth, and reliable. For a broader starting point, see [best fountain pens for beginners](/magazine/best-fountain-pens-beginners).

**2. Do Japanese nibs really run that much finer than Western ones?**
Yes. Plan on going one size broader than your Western habit. A Japanese Medium ≈ Western Fine.

**3. Which brand has the smoothest nibs?**
Pilot, comfortably. If "writes like glass" is your goal, start there.

**4. Which brand is best if I leave pens sitting unused?**
Platinum, because of the Slip & Seal cap. A 3776 will start instantly after weeks of sitting.

**5. Can I use any bottled ink in these pens?**
Yes, with a converter. The proprietary *cartridges* differ between brands, but a converter lets you draw any bottled ink you like.

**6. Which has the best ink capacity?**
Pilot's Custom 823 vacuum filler, by a wide margin. If you write a lot before refilling, it is the clear winner.

**7. Is Sailor's feedback the same as scratchiness?**
No. Scratchiness is a fault (a misaligned or rough nib). Sailor feedback is a deliberate, smooth-but-textured feel. A well-tuned Sailor is never scratchy.

**8. Are gold nibs worth it over these brands' steel nibs?**
Sometimes. Steel nibs from all three (Metropolitan, Plaisir, etc.) are excellent and cost a fraction. Gold buys you a softer, more refined feel — a luxury, not a necessity. The [gold nib vs steel nib](/magazine/gold-nib-vs-steel-nib) guide covers the trade-off in detail.

## Final Thoughts

The big three are not ranked — they are three different answers to the same question. Sailor gives you texture and craft. Pilot gives you smoothness and engineering. Platinum gives you precision and the best ink-keeping in the business. Buy the one whose personality matches yours, mind the finer Japanese sizing, and you will be hard-pressed to make a bad choice among them.
